A property of every projection

P projects onto the span of the columns of A, and r = v − P v. Which statement always holds?

‖r‖ = ‖P v‖
P has an inverse
P r = r
Aᵀ r = 0
Why. The residual of an orthogonal projection is orthogonal to every column; in fact P r = 0.

Answer D. Aᵀ r = 0 is about to become the normal equations.

Block A · least squares

With more data points than unknowns, there is usually no exact solution

  • Three points, a line with two coefficients: X β = y has no solution unless the points line up
  • Least squares chooses β to make ‖y − X β‖ as small as possible
  • X β ranges over the span of the columns, so the best X β is the projection of y onto it
So: the residual y − X β is orthogonal to every column: Xᵀ(y − X β) = 0.

Draw y outside the plane of the columns and its shadow X β. Every regression a participant has run did exactly this.

The normal equations

XᵀX β = Xᵀy: least squares by hand

Points (−1, 1), (0, 1), (1, 4); X = [1, x]

XᵀX = [[3, 0], [0, 2]], Xᵀy = (6, 3)

β = (2, 1.5): y ≈ 2 + 1.5x

Check the residual

Fitted (0.5, 2, 3.5), residual (0.5, −1, 0.5). It sums to zero and is orthogonal to x: Xᵀr = (0, 0).

From slide_numbers.py, week 5. x was chosen centred, so XᵀX is diagonal and the arithmetic is easy. Point that out: centring helps by hand, and it helps the computer too (block C).

The QR route

With X = QR, least squares becomes a triangular solve: R β = Qᵀy

Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X)
beta = solve_triangular(R, Q.T @ y)

# the library's own route (SVD)
beta = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
  • Q Qᵀ y is the projection of y; Qᵀy its coordinates
  • XᵀX is never formed
  • Back substitution, as in week 3

Derive it: XᵀX = RᵀQᵀQR = RᵀR, so the normal equations become RᵀR β = RᵀQᵀy, and R is invertible when the columns are independent. Same answer in exact arithmetic; the difference is rounding, block B.

Checkpoint

Which fit is the least-squares fit?

Two candidate fits for the same data. Fit F has residual (1, −2, 1) and fit G has residual (2, −1, −1). The columns of X are (1, 1, 1) and (−1, 0, 1). Which can be the least-squares fit?

G, because its residual is more even
F, because its residual is orthogonal to both columns
Both, since their residuals have the same length
Neither, because neither residual is zero
Why. F: sums to 0 and (1, −2, 1) · (−1, 0, 1) = 0. G: (2, −1, −1) · (−1, 0, 1) = −3, not orthogonal.

Answer B. Both residuals have norm √6: equal length does not make G optimal.

Block B · conditioning

The condition number says how much a small change in the data can move the answer

A = [[1, 1], [1, 1.0001]], cond(A) ≈ 4 × 10⁴

bSolution of A x = b
(2, 2.0001)(1, 1)
(2, 2.0002)(0, 2)

Rule of thumb

About log₁₀ cond decimal digits of accuracy are at risk. Float64 carries about 16.

From slide_numbers.py, week 5. A change of 0.0001 in one entry of b moved the solution from (1, 1) to (0, 2). The two columns are nearly parallel: the system cannot tell them apart.

Why QR

Forming XᵀX squares the condition number

4 × 10⁴
cond(A) for the matrix of the last slide
1.6 × 10⁹
cond(AᵀA): about 9 of 16 digits at risk
The normal equations work with cond(X)²; QR works with cond(X). For well-conditioned data both are fine. As columns approach collinearity, the normal equations fail first.

From slide_numbers.py, week 5. The lab measures where on the course data the difference shows and where it does not. Do not promise a dramatic failure in float64 at moderate conditioning: there is none.

In data

Nearly collinear features make coefficients unstable and fitted values stable

  • Two columns that nearly repeat each other: many β give almost the same X β
  • The fit barely changes when the data is nudged
  • The split of the effect between the two columns can swing widely
For the report. Do not read the coefficient of one of two nearly collinear features as "the effect" of that feature.

Example to discuss, not the lab's: house area in square metres and in square feet with a small recording error. The model predicts prices well and cannot say which unit "matters".

Checkpoint

Diagnose a fit

A fit has cond(X) = 10⁷. Refitting after rounding the data to four decimals changes two coefficients by 30% and the fitted values by 0.01%. What is the diagnosis?

The rounding was too coarse; the model is fine
The model is stable, since its predictions hardly moved
Ill-conditioned: nearly collinear columns; trust the fit, not those two coefficients
Rank-deficient: one column must be removed before any fit
Why. Full rank, high condition number, stable predictions, unstable coefficients: the pattern of near-collinearity.

Answer C. This is the kind of case the LO3 diagnosis sheet uses: rank, condition number and what moves.

Block C · what helps

Some ill-conditioning is only a matter of units

CauseWhat helps
Columns on very different scales (metres and millimetres)Divide each column by its standard deviation
A large mean far from zero, beside the interceptCentre each column
Columns that nearly repeat each otherDrop or combine one; scaling does not help
Exactly repeated columnsDrop one: the matrix is rank-deficient

The lab's stretch measures the first two on its data. Scaling changes the units of the coefficients: say so in the report.

In practice

Prefer QR or the library's least-squares solver; check the condition number

Normal equations

Fast and simple; squares the condition number. Fine when cond(X) is small.

QR

The default for full-rank problems: works with cond(X).

SVD (lstsq)

Slowest; handles rank deficiency by ignoring tiny singular values. Week 7.

np.linalg.lstsq uses the SVD. SciPy's lstsq offers several drivers; library defaults change, so check the documentation of the version you use.

A feature matrix has cond(X) = 10⁶ in float64. You need coefficients accurate to at least six digits. Which route?

The normal equations: they are fastest
QR, which puts about 6 digits at risk and leaves about 10
The normal equations in float32 to save memory
Invert XᵀX and multiply by Xᵀy
Why. QR works with cond(X) = 10⁶: about 16 − 6 = 10 digits remain.

Week 5 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Least squares is projection

The residual is orthogonal to the columns: Xᵀ(y − X β) = 0.

XᵀX squares trouble

cond(XᵀX) = cond(X)². Use QR or the library's solver.

Fits outlast coefficients

Near-collinearity leaves predictions stable and individual coefficients unreliable.
