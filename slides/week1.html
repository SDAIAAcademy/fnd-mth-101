Block A · vectors

A vector is a point and an arrow at the same time

A point: a data record

A learner's week as (videos, quizzes, posts) = (1, 2, 2). Each coordinate is one measured quantity.

An arrow: a direction

The same numbers drawn from the origin. Its direction says the mix of activity; its length says how much.

Order matters. (1, 2, 2) and (2, 1, 2) hold the same numbers and are different vectors: the coordinates are named positions.

Draw the vector (1, 2) on the board as a point and as an arrow. Ask: what would a learner with (2, 4) look like? Same direction, twice as long: the same mix, twice the activity.

Two operations

Adding vectors adds coordinates; scaling stretches the arrow

OperationExampleResultGeometry
Sum(1, 2, 2) + (2, 1, 2)(3, 3, 4)Head to tail
Scale3 × (1, 2, 2)(3, 6, 6)Same direction, three times as long
Difference(2, 1, 2) − (1, 2, 2)(1, −1, 0)The arrow from the first point to the second

In NumPy each line is one expression on arrays: x + y, 3 * x, y - x.

Work the three rows on the board before showing the result column. Stress the difference vector: it is what a distance is measured on, next slide.

The norm

The norm is the length of the arrow

‖x‖ = √(x₁² + x₂² + x₃²), Pythagoras in three dimensions.

For x = (1, 2, 2): ‖x‖ = √(1 + 4 + 4) = √9 = 3.

The distance between two points is the norm of their difference: ‖y − x‖ = ‖(1, −1, 0)‖ = √2 ≈ 1.41.

Units

A norm adds squares of coordinates. If one coordinate is minutes and another is counts, the minutes dominate. Scale before you measure.

Numbers from labs/_tools/slide_numbers.py, week 1. Ask the room for ‖3x‖ before revealing: 9, three times 3. Scaling a vector by c scales its norm by |c|.

Checkpoint

Which learner is nearest?

Learners are vectors of (video minutes, quizzes). Learner K is (300, 2). Which is nearest to K by Euclidean distance: M (305, 9), N (330, 2) or P (290, 2)?

P, because it matches K exactly on quizzes
N, because its only difference is in one coordinate
M, at a distance of about 8.6
None: minutes and quizzes cannot be combined in one distance
Why. M: √(5² + 7²) ≈ 8.6; P: 10; N: 30. M wins although it took seven more quizzes, because one quiz counts as much as one minute here. Whether that is sensible is a scaling decision.

Answer C. Then ask: should one quiz weigh as much as one minute? This is why features are scaled before distances are trusted; week 5 returns to scaling.

Block B · the dot product

The dot product multiplies matching coordinates and adds them

x = (1, 2, 2), y = (2, 1, 2)

x · y = 1×2 + 2×1 + 2×2 = 8

And x · x = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 = ‖x‖²: the norm is a dot product with itself.

x = np.array([1, 2, 2])
y = np.array([2, 1, 2])
x @ y              # 8
np.sqrt(x @ x)     # 3.0

Have everyone compute x · y on paper before the code appears. The @ operator is NumPy's product; on two vectors it is the dot product.

Geometry

x · y = ‖x‖ ‖y‖ cos θ: the dot product measures an angle

For our pair: cos θ = 8 / (3 × 3) = 0.889, so θ ≈ 27.3°.

The cosine similarity is that cosine: the dot product of the two vectors after each is scaled to length 1.

0.889
cosine of (1, 2, 2) and (2, 1, 2)

Numbers from slide_numbers.py, week 1. The formula follows from the law of cosines on the triangle formed by x, y and y − x; derive it if the group is comfortable, otherwise state it and check it on the example.

Reading the sign

The sign of a dot product says acute, right or obtuse

PairDot productAngleReading
(1, 0) and (1, 1)145°Pointing the same way, partly
(1, 0) and (0, 1)090°Orthogonal: nothing in common
(1, 0) and (−1, 1)−1135°Pointing partly opposite
Orthogonal means dot product zero. It comes back in every week from week 4 on.

Sketch the three pairs. Ask: if two learners' activity vectors are orthogonal, what do they share? Nothing: each does what the other does not.

Length or direction

Cosine ignores length; the dot product does not

x and 3x

x · 3x = 27, larger than x · y = 8. Yet cos(x, 3x) = 1: the same direction exactly.

Why a search cares

A long document has a long vector. Ranking by raw dot product would favour length over meaning. Cosine compares only direction.

From slide_numbers.py: x · 3x = 27, cosine 1. Ask which of the two a help-centre search should use, and why. Cosine, unless length itself carries meaning.

At scale

A matrix times a vector is many dot products at once

D = np.array([[1, 2, 2],
              [2, 1, 2],
              [3, 6, 6]], float)
N = D / np.linalg.norm(D, axis=1, keepdims=True)
q = np.array([1, 2, 2]) / 3
N @ q      # [1.0, 0.889, 1.0]
  • Each row of D is one item; normalise every row to length 1
  • N @ q gives the cosine of the question with every row
  • The best match is the largest entry: np.argmax

Run it live. Point out that rows 1 and 3 tie at 1.0: the same direction, different lengths. This is the search the lab builds, on 60 help articles instead of three rows.

Checkpoint

Dot product or cosine?

A question vector q = (1, 1). Two help articles: A = (4, 4) and B = (1, 1.2). Which statement is right?

A is closer in direction, since q · A = 8 beats q · B = 2.2
A and q differ in direction, since A is four times longer
Both measures rank A first, so the choice never matters
A has cosine 1 with q and B a little less; the dot product also rewards A's length
Why. A = 4q, so cos(q, A) = 1; B is slightly off q's direction. Both measures put A first here, for different reasons.

Answer D. cos(q, B) = 2.2 / (1.414 × 1.562) ≈ 0.996. If the room picks C, give a counter-example: q = (1, 0), A = (5, 5), B = (1, 0.1). Dot product puts A first, cosine puts B first.

Block C · embeddings

An embedding places words so that related words point the same way

  • A model reads a large text collection and gives each word a vector
  • Words used in similar contexts get vectors at small angles
  • The course uses GloVe: 400,000 words, 50 numbers each, trained on 6 billion tokens of encyclopedia and news text

Licence

The pre-trained GloVe vectors are released under the Public Domain Dedication and License. The lab downloads them; nothing is copied into the course.

Sources: the GloVe repository README (licence) and the gensim-data listing (file, size, checksum). The vectors are from 2014 text: an old, small model, chosen because it is public, light and runs on a laptop. Modern sentence models are better, and the geometry is the same.

From words to sentences

Here a sentence vector is the mean of its word vectors

1 · Split"video will not play" into four words
→
2 · Look upfour GloVe vectors of 50 numbers
→
3 · Averageone vector of 50 numbers for the sentence
A baseline, not a sentence model. The mean ignores word order and is pulled by frequent words. It is the simplest public sentence embedding, and the lab measures how far it gets.

Be plain about this: the course does not call a sentence model, because that would mean downloading one or using a paid service. The mean of public word vectors is a documented baseline, and its weaknesses are part of the lesson.

What the vectors learnt

Vectors learn the associations of the text they were trained on

The lean of a word w between two word lists:

lean(w) = mean of cos(w, female word) − cos(w, male word), over pairs such as she/he and woman/man.

Positive: closer to the female words. Zero: no lean.

Why it matters

A search that matches a learner's free-text goal to courses through these vectors inherits their leans. The lab measures them; whether a search treats learners differently must be tested on the search.

Define the measure only; the lab computes it on 14 occupation words. Ask what a lean near zero would mean, and what it would not prove (it would not prove a search is fair: other words could carry the same association).

Checkpoint

Reading a search score

A search over mean word vectors returns two articles with cosines 0.951 and 0.949 to a question. What is the soundest reading?

The scores barely differ; check whether a shared direction inflates every cosine before trusting the order
Both are excellent matches, since a cosine above 0.9 means near-identical meaning
The first article is clearly the better answer, since it scores higher
There is a 95% chance that either article answers the question
Why. High, nearly equal cosines are what a shared direction produces. Centring the vectors and comparing again shows whether the order means anything.

Answer A. Link back to the cone slide. This is exactly what the lab investigates in step 5.

Week 1 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Length and angle

The norm is a length; the dot product divided by two lengths is the cosine of an angle.

Centre first

A direction every vector shares makes every cosine high. Subtract the mean before comparing.

Vectors carry text

Embeddings inherit the associations of their training text. Measure them before building on them.

Ask each participant to say which of the three they would explain to a colleague first, and how.
